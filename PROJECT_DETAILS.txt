================================================================================
                        NIVRA - THE MASTER GUIDE
================================================================================

What this file is: the single place to understand everything about Nivra -
what it is, why it exists, every architectural choice, why it was made, what
alternatives were rejected, and what each choice led to. It is a living
document: every decision we make gets added here.

How to read it: each idea is a short paragraph of 3-4 lines. Sections go from
"why" (problem, landscape) to "what" (product) to "how" (architecture), and end
with a Decision Log that records every choice in order with its status.

Status tags used throughout:
  [DECIDED]   - agreed by the team, build on it
  [PROPOSED]  - recommended, waiting for team sign-off
  [OPEN]      - still a question, no recommendation yet
  [REJECTED]  - considered and turned down (kept so we remember why)

Last updated: 2026-11-14 (project kickoff)


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1.1 What happens to victims
Someone's photo - often intimate, sometimes ordinary - gets uploaded online
without their consent. It may be on a social network, a porn site, a Telegram
channel, or a forum. The person usually finds out late, feels panic and shame,
and has no idea who to contact or what their rights are.

1.2 Why removal is hard today
Every platform has a different reporting form, different rules, and different
response times. Laws differ by country. Offshore sites ignore requests. Victims
must repeatedly look at the content to report it, which re-traumatises them.
Even after removal, the same image gets re-uploaded elsewhere.

1.3 The core idea of Nivra
Nivra is a guided helper that takes a victim from "I just found my photo
online" to "it's been taken down and I'm watching for re-uploads". It finds
the right reporting channel, writes the legal notice for them, tracks every
link, and escalates when platforms don't respond.

1.4 Kinds of non-consensual images in scope [DECIDED - see D1]
(a) Intimate images shared without consent (NCII, "revenge porn").
(b) Morphed / AI-generated deepfakes using someone's face.
(c) Ordinary photos misused for impersonation - fake profiles, catfishing.
All three are in scope for adults. They share one takedown flow; only the
legal basis and wording of the notice change.

1.5 Children are a special case [DECIDED]
If the person in the image was under 18 when it was taken, Nivra does NOT
handle the image at all. It routes the user to NCMEC's Take It Down, the
cybercrime.gov.in "child pornography/CSAM" category and the 1930 helpline.
Reason: even holding such material is a crime, and these channels have legal
powers we don't.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE EXISTING LANDSCAPE (what already exists and how Nivra differs)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.1 StopNCII.org
Run by SWGfL with Meta's tech. The user's device computes a hash (digital
fingerprint) of the image; only the hash is shared with partner platforms
(Meta, TikTok, Reddit, Bing and others), which block matching uploads. Great
for prevention on big platforms, useless for small/offshore sites.

2.2 Take It Down (NCMEC)
Same hash idea, but for images of people taken when they were under 18. Nivra
must route minors here instead of handling their images itself - possessing
such images is a serious crime even for a helper service.

2.3 Platform and search-engine forms
Google, Bing, Meta, X, Reddit etc. each have NCII/privacy removal forms.
Google's de-indexing form is especially powerful: even if a site refuses to
remove the image, it can disappear from search results, which is where most
people would find it.

2.4 Where the gap is (Nivra's reason to exist)
Existing tools are scattered, each covers one platform or one type of victim,
and none tracks a case end-to-end. Nivra's value is orchestration: one place
that knows every channel, writes every notice, follows up, escalates, and
watches for re-uploads.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE LEGAL GROUND WE STAND ON
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.1 India - IT Rules 2021
Rule 3(2)(b) requires intermediaries to remove content showing a person's
private area, nudity, sexual acts or impersonation (incl. morphed images)
within 24 hours of a complaint by that person or someone on their behalf.
Every significant platform must publish a Grievance Officer contact.

3.2 India - escalation routes
If a platform ignores the grievance, users can appeal to the Grievance
Appellate Committee (GAC). Criminal complaints go to cybercrime.gov.in or the
1930 helpline. IT Act Sections 66E and 67/67A cover these offences.

3.3 USA - TAKE IT DOWN Act (2025)
Makes publishing NCII (including AI deepfakes) a federal crime and requires
covered platforms to remove reported NCII within 48 hours of a valid request.
DMCA copyright notices also work when the victim took the photo themselves
(a selfie's copyright belongs to the person who took it).

3.4 EU / UK
GDPR's right to erasure and the EU Digital Services Act's notice-and-action
system apply to EU users. The UK Online Safety Act makes sharing intimate
images an offence and places duties on platforms.

3.5 Our legal limits
Nivra is not a law firm. We generate templates and explain options; we do not
give legal advice. Notices are sent in the user's name with their explicit
consent. False takedown notices can carry penalties, so verification matters.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. KEY CHALLENGES (and how we plan to handle each)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

4.1 Finding where the image is
There is no free, legal, public API for reverse image search at scale. Google
Lens has no API, Bing's search APIs were retired in 2025, TinEye's API is paid,
and crawling adult sites ourselves is expensive and legally grey. Plan: start
with user-supplied links plus assisted reverse search (see 5.1).

4.2 Holding sensitive images is a liability
If victims upload intimate images to our server, a breach would be a disaster,
and if any image shows a minor we'd be holding illegal material. Plan: images
never leave the user's device. We compute hashes and do any processing in the
browser. The server only stores links, statuses and hashes.

4.3 Misuse of the tool itself
A stalker could use a "find my photos" feature to find someone else's photos,
or a bad actor could file fake takedowns against legitimate content. Plan:
no open face-search, notices sent only for content the user attests is
theirs, rate limits, and (later) identity checks for automated features.

4.4 Every platform is different
Reporting channels are web forms, emails, in-app buttons - almost never APIs.
Auto-filling forms breaks platform ToS and hits CAPTCHAs. Plan: a curated
Platform Directory + pre-filled notices the user submits in one click, and
direct emails to grievance/abuse addresses where they exist.

4.5 Non-cooperative / offshore sites
Some sites ignore every request. Plan: an escalation ladder - site, then the
hosting provider, the CDN (e.g. Cloudflare), the domain registrar, search
engine de-indexing, and finally law enforcement. Each step is automatable.

4.6 Re-uploads
Removed images reappear. Plan: periodic re-checking of known links, plus
hash registration with StopNCII for participating platforms, plus (later)
monitoring for new copies.

4.7 Evidence vs. removal conflict
Police and lawyers need evidence, but the goal is removal. If we remove first,
evidence is lost. Plan: an Evidence Locker that captures URL, timestamp,
screenshot and hash before takedown, encrypted on the user's side.

4.8 Trauma and emotional safety
Users may be in crisis. Plan: trauma-informed design - calm language, blurred
previews by default, never forcing the user to view the content, a quick-exit
button, helpline links on every screen, and an option to let a trusted friend
handle the case.

4.9 Deepfakes and morphed images
A morphed image isn't a copy of an original photo, so hash matching fails.
Detection needs face-based matching, which is biometric data and sensitive
under India's DPDP Act and laws like Illinois' BIPA. Plan: handle deepfakes
through the same takedown flow first; face matching only later, opt-in.

4.10 Cost and scale
We are a small team. Paid search APIs, crawling and ML inference cost money.
Plan: build an MVP that is almost free to run (user links + notice generation
+ tracking) and add expensive discovery features only once the core works.

5. APPROACHES CONSIDERED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

5.1 Discovery (finding the images)
  A. User-provided links - user pastes URLs they found. Zero legal risk, cheap,
     works today. Limitation: only finds what the user already knows.
  B. Assisted reverse search - the user picks an image on their own device and
     we open Google Lens / Yandex / TinEye with it. They search, we organise
     the results. Note: this does send the image to that search engine, and
     the user must be told so.
  C. Hash registration - integrate with / redirect to StopNCII rather than
     build our own hash network, since platforms already trust it.
  D. Own crawler + perceptual hash matching - the "dream" feature, but costly
     and legally risky. Later phase, if ever.
  E. Face search (PimEyes-style) - highest misuse risk. Only ever as a
     verified self-search, and probably never in MVP.
  Recommendation [PROPOSED]: A + B + C for MVP. D/E deferred.

5.2 Takedown (getting it removed)
  A. Notice generator - picks the strongest legal basis (IT Rules, DMCA,
     TAKE IT DOWN, GDPR, platform policy) and fills a template.
  B. Platform Directory - for each platform/host: reporting URL, email,
     grievance officer, legal basis, expected response time.
  C. Escalation ladder - automatic suggestions when deadlines pass.
  D. Case tracker - each link has a status; we re-check if it's still live.
  E. Sending on the user's behalf - emails from Nivra with consent. Needs
     good email deliverability and clear authorisation wording.
  Recommendation [PROPOSED]: A + B + C + D for MVP, E soon after.

6. PRODUCT FORM FACTOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

6.1 Website - the core product [DECIDED - see D4]
Nivra is a website, not an app. It opens in any browser on phone or laptop
with nothing to install. Pages are responsive (they adapt to small screens)
because many victims will find Nivra on their phone, often in a hurry.
We deliberately do not push "install this app" (no PWA, no app store).

6.1a Why a website and not an app
No install means no app icon on the phone for someone else to spot, which
matters for a victim's privacy. It is also one codebase, needs no app-store
review, and can be shared as a simple link by NGOs, friends or police.

6.2 Chrome extension - companion [PROPOSED, phase 2]
Right-click any image on any page -> "Report with Nivra". It captures the
URL, takes an evidence screenshot, hashes the image locally, and adds it to
the user's case. Excellent for collecting links, but desktop-only, so it
cannot be the main product.

6.3 Agent skill - not the product [REJECTED as main form]
An AI-assistant skill alone would feel low effort and puts a sensitive flow
inside someone else's product. However, an AI agent INSIDE Nivra's backend
(writing notices, finding host contacts, reading platform replies) is
valuable and is planned.

6.4 Messaging bot (Telegram / WhatsApp) [OPEN, later]
A lot of leaked content in India spreads on Telegram. A bot that accepts a
forwarded link and opens a case meets users where the harm happens. Worth
considering after the web app is stable.

6.5 Mobile app / PWA [REJECTED]
App-store review, two codebases, installation friction, and a visible icon
on the victim's phone. The team prefers a pure website; a responsive site
covers phone users without any of these downsides.

7. FEATURE IDEAS (beyond the obvious)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

7.1 Quick exit button - one tap jumps to a harmless page (e.g. weather) and
clears the tab, in case someone walks in on the user.

7.2 Anonymous mode - open a case with no email/phone, just a passphrase.
Lowers the barrier for people afraid of being identified.

7.3 Trusted helper mode - the victim can hand the case to a friend, family
member or NGO worker who does the work without the victim seeing the content.

7.4 Evidence Locker - encrypted on the user's device (we can't read it),
exportable as a PDF evidence pack with timestamps and SHA-256 hashes for
police or lawyers.

7.5 Re-upload watch - Nivra periodically re-checks removed links and alerts
the user if content comes back.

7.6 Platform response scoreboard - aggregated, anonymous stats of which
platforms remove content fastest. Useful for users and for advocacy.

7.7 Multilingual - Hindi and other Indian languages, both for the UI and for
notices where local language helps.

7.8 NGO / legal-aid partners - referral links to support organisations,
counselling, and pro-bono lawyers built into the flow.

7.9 Prevention tools [later] - helping people protect photos before posting
(e.g. perturbation techniques that make AI morphing harder).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. ARCHITECTURE (MVP) [DECIDED - see D3, D6]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

8.1 Guiding principle: data minimisation
The server should know as little as possible. No images, only links, hashes,
statuses and the minimum contact info needed. If we're ever breached, the
leaked data should be close to useless to an attacker.

8.2 Frontend
Next.js (React) + TypeScript + Tailwind CSS, as a responsive website. Next.js
gives us routing, server rendering for fast first load on slow phones, and
API routes in the same project. TypeScript catches bugs early.

8.3 Client-side image processing
Perceptual hashing (pHash / PDQ) and encryption run in the browser using
WebAssembly and the Web Crypto API. This is what lets us promise "your images
never leave your device".

8.4 Backend
Start inside Next.js API routes to keep one codebase. If we add heavy image or
ML work later, a separate Python (FastAPI) service can be added for that part
only. Background jobs handle link re-checks and reminders.

8.5 Database
PostgreSQL (e.g. via Supabase) for cases, links, platform directory and
statuses. Relational data fits well: a case has many links, each link belongs
to a platform, each platform has many reporting channels.

8.6 AI layer
An LLM (Claude) drafts notices tailored to platform + law, classifies a URL
to its platform/host, and later reads platform replies to update status.
Images are never sent to the LLM - only text and URLs.

8.7 Email
A transactional email service (e.g. Resend / Postmark) for sending notices
on the user's behalf and for status updates, with proper SPF/DKIM so mails
don't land in spam.

9. FRONTEND DESIGN DIRECTION (draft, to be refined with the design prompt)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

9.1 Tone - calm, private, in control. Soft neutral colours, no stock images
of people, no alarming red. The user should feel safe the moment the page
loads.

9.2 Main screens
Landing -> "Start a case" wizard (what happened -> where is it -> evidence ->

9.3 Safety UX - quick exit always visible, blurred thumbnails by default,

10. MVP BLUEPRINT (what we build first and how the pieces fit)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

10.1 The MVP in one sentence
A user pastes the links where their image appears; Nivra identifies each
platform, writes the right legal notice, shows exactly where to send it,
tracks every link's status, and tells the user what to do when a platform
doesn't respond.

10.2 The main user journey ("Start a case" wizard)
Step 1 Safety check - is the user safe right now? Was the person under 18?
  (Under 18 -> redirect to Take It Down / cybercrime.gov.in, flow ends.)
Step 2 What happened - intimate image, morphed/deepfake, or fake profile.
Step 3 Where is it - paste one or more links (more can be added later).
Step 4 Evidence - guided screenshot + timestamp capture before removal.
Step 5 Notices - Nivra drafts one notice per link; user reviews and sends.
Step 6 Save case - user gets a Case ID + chooses a passphrase to return.

10.3 Case identity (anonymous accounts, see D7)
A case is identified by a random Case ID (e.g. NV-7K3Q-92XD) and a
passphrase the user chooses. The server stores only a slow hash of the
passphrase (Argon2), never the passphrase itself. Lose both = lose the case,
which is the honest trade-off for anonymity; we say this clearly.

10.4 What lives where [DECIDED - D9]
Links, statuses and notice text are stored on the server, because the server
must re-check links and send reminders. Evidence (screenshots) is encrypted
on the user's device with a key derived from their passphrase, so even if
it's backed up to our server, we cannot read it.

10.5 Link status lifecycle
found -> reported -> awaiting response -> removed
                                   \-> no response (deadline passed) -> escalated
                                   \-> rejected -> escalated
removed -> reappeared (re-check found it live again) -> reported ...
Every status change is stored as an event, so the user sees a full timeline
and we can build the evidence PDF from it.

10.6 Platform resolution
When a link is added, Nivra reads its domain and looks it up in the Platform
Directory (instagram.com -> Meta). If unknown, it looks up the hosting
provider (DNS/IP -> host's abuse contact) so there is always someone to
send a notice to. Unknown domains are logged so we can add them over time.

10.7 Data model (database tables)
  cases           - id, passphrase_hash, category, created_at, email (opt.)
  links           - id, case_id, url, platform_id, status, last_checked_at
  status_events   - id, link_id, from_status, to_status, note, created_at
  notices         - id, link_id, legal_basis, channel_id, body, sent_at
  platforms       - id, name, domains[], country, notes
  report_channels - id, platform_id, type (form/email/in-app), target,
                    legal_basis, expected_response_hours
A case has many links; a link has many events and notices; a platform has
many report channels (e.g. Meta has an NCII form AND a grievance email).

10.8 Legal basis modules
Each law is a module: India IT Rules, US TAKE IT DOWN, US DMCA, EU GDPR,
platform policy. A module says when it applies (country, category, who took
the photo), its deadline, and its notice template. The notice generator
picks the strongest applicable module for each link.

10.9 Pages (routes)
  /                    landing - what Nivra does, privacy promise, start
  /start               the case wizard (10.2)
  /case                open an existing case (Case ID + passphrase)
  /case/[id]           case dashboard - links, statuses, next actions
  /guides              platform guides directory
  /guides/[platform]   how to report on one platform, step by step
  /help                helplines, NGOs, legal aid, police reporting
  Quick exit (every page) -> replaces the tab with a neutral site.

10.10 API (server endpoints)
  POST /api/cases                 create case, returns Case ID
  POST /api/cases/open            verify Case ID + passphrase, start session
  POST /api/cases/[id]/links      add links, resolves platform
  PATCH /api/links/[id]           update status (e.g. user marks "sent")
  POST /api/notices/draft         generate notice text for a link
  GET  /api/platforms/resolve     url -> platform + report channels

10.11 Repository layout [DECIDED - D10]
  /web          the Next.js app (frontend + API)
  /extension    Chrome extension (phase 2, empty for now)
  PROJECT_DETAILS.txt  this guide
Why: keeping the web app in its own folder leaves room for the extension
(and maybe a Telegram bot) in the same repo without a messy restructure.

10.12 Build order
  Phase 1  Scaffold, landing page, design system, quick exit, help page.
  Phase 2  Case wizard + Platform Directory (seeded with top ~20 platforms).
  Phase 3  Database, anonymous cases, case dashboard, status tracking.
  Phase 4  Notice generation (templates first, Claude-assisted after).
  Phase 5  Link re-checks, escalation suggestions, evidence PDF export.
  Phase 6  Chrome extension, optional email alerts, Hindi.


10.13 Scaffold - what was set up and why (2026-11-14)
The web app was created with create-next-app: Next.js 16, React 19,
TypeScript, Tailwind CSS 4, ESLint, the App Router and a src/ folder.
The App Router is Next's current routing system - each folder in
src/app becomes a page, which maps cleanly onto the routes in 10.9.

10.14 Note for anyone (or any AI) editing the code
Next.js 16 has breaking changes from older versions. web/AGENTS.md (created
by Next.js itself) says to check the docs bundled in
node_modules/next/dist/docs/ before writing code, not to rely on memory.

10.14a Hydration warning from browser extensions (fixed 2026-11-14)
A custom-cursor browser extension added attributes to <html> before React
loaded, so Next.js showed a "hydration mismatch" error. It was not our bug.
We added suppressHydrationWarning to <html> and <body> in layout.tsx; it
ignores only those two tags' own attributes. Victims use extensions too.

10.15a Working prototype frontend (built 2026-11-14)
A clickable prototype of the whole first-version journey now exists. You
can start a case, paste links, get notices, track statuses, see escalation
steps, lock the case and reopen it. It runs entirely in the browser with no
server or database yet, so it can be tested today.

10.15b Design direction - "Garden" [DECIDED - D11]
Warm oat paper, deep green ink, Vollkorn serif headings over Hanken Grotesk
body text, hairline rules, one leaf-green colour for the main action. It
feels calm, human and trustworthy - not clinical, not "tech startup", and
not alarming. No photos of people anywhere, on purpose (victims of image
abuse should never be shown faces or bodies).

10.15c The signature: the Case ID slip
The one thing a visitor should remember is the Case ID card (e.g.
NV-BGUH-9K9R). It is shown on the landing page and the dashboard, because it
captures Nivra's promise: you get help without giving your name.

10.15d Where the code lives (web/src)
  app/page.tsx              landing page
  app/start/StartWizard.tsx the 6-step "Start a case" flow
  app/case/CaseView.tsx     unlock form + case dashboard
  app/guides/...            platform directory and per-platform guides
  app/help/page.tsx         helplines, police reporting, under-18 routes
  components/               header, footer, quick-exit button
  lib/platforms.ts          Platform Directory + link -> platform lookup
  lib/notices.ts            legal grounds + notice template generator
  lib/caseStore.ts          prototype case storage (see 10.15e)
  lib/types.ts              shared data types (matches blueprint 10.7)
  app/globals.css           the whole design system as CSS tokens

10.15e Prototype storage - temporary [DECIDED - D12]
Cases are stored in the browser's localStorage, and the open case in
sessionStorage (closing the tab locks it). Passphrases are hashed with
PBKDF2 (150,000 rounds) and never stored as typed. This is a stand-in:
the real product stores links on a server (D9) so it can re-check them.

10.15f Quick exit
The dark "Quick exit" button is on every page, and pressing Esc twice does
the same. It clears the session and replaces the tab with a Google weather
search using location.replace, so pressing Back does not return to Nivra.

10.15g How links are recognised
Pasted text is split one link per line (or comma). Each link's domain is
matched against the directory, including subdomains (m.facebook.com ->
Facebook). Unknown sites get generic steps: contact page, hosting company,
Cloudflare abuse, Google de-indexing.

10.15h How notices are written
Every notice cites IT Rules 2021 Rule 3(2)(b) (24-hour removal), plus IT Act
sections matching the category (66E/67/67A for intimate and morphed images,
66C/66D for impersonation), plus copyright if the user took the photo. They
are templates, not legal advice, and need a lawyer's review before launch.

10.15i Known prototype limits (must fix before launch)
- Platform links, steps and helpline numbers are unverified prototype data.
- Cases live in one browser only; clearing browser data deletes them.
- "Pretend the deadline passed" is a testing button and must be removed.
- No server, so no automatic re-checks, reminders or email sending yet.

10.15j How to test as a user
Run "npm run dev" inside web/, open localhost:3000, click Start a case and
go through every step with fake links (e.g. instagram.com/p/test1,
t.me/test/1, randomsite.xyz/a). On the dashboard, mark a link as sent, press
the prototype deadline button, and check that the escalation steps appear.

10.15 Branching workflow
main always holds working code. Each piece of work happens on its own branch
(e.g. setup/mvp-architecture), gets pushed, and is merged into main through
a pull request. This keeps a clear history of why each change was made.

D0  [DECIDED] Repository: github.com/HunarBhatia/NIVRA, local folder
    Desktop/NIVRA. All code is pushed here.

D1  [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Scope: intimate images, deepfakes/morphed images,
    and impersonation (fake profiles) - for adults. Minors are routed to
    Take It Down / cybercrime.gov.in, never handled by Nivra.
    Why: all three share the same takedown mechanics, so covering them costs
    little extra, and morphed images are a fast-growing problem in India.
    Excluding children's images keeps us legally safe.

D2  [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Geography: India first, built to go global.
    Why: Indian law (IT Rules 24-hour removal) is strong and few tools serve
    Indian users. Laws are kept as separate "legal basis" modules (India,
    US, EU...) so adding a country means adding a module, not a rewrite.

D3  [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Images never leave the user's device; the server
    stores no images, only links, hashes and statuses.
    Why: removes breach risk and the risk of holding illegal material. It
    also makes "launch for real" possible without a legal team.

D4  [DECIDED 2026-11-14, revised same day] Form factor: a website that
    works well on phones and laptops - NOT an app or PWA (team preference;
    also no tell-tale app icon on a victim's phone). Chrome
    extension in phase 2. Agent skill rejected as the main product.

D5  [DECIDED 2026-11-14] MVP discovery: user-pasted links + assisted reverse
    image search + StopNCII referral. No own crawler or face search in MVP.
    Why: zero legal risk and near-zero cost; expensive discovery is only
    worth building once the takedown flow is proven.

D6  [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Stack: Next.js (App Router) + TypeScript + Tailwind
    + PostgreSQL + Claude for notice drafting.
    Why: one codebase for frontend and API, large ecosystem, easy hosting,
    and strong typing for a sensitive app where bugs are costly.

D7  [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Accounts: anonymous by default (case ID +
    passphrase), optional email added later for reminders and alerts.
    Why: fear of being identified is a big reason victims don't seek help.
    Email is opt-in for those who want follow-up notifications.

D8  [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Goal: build to real-launch quality, released in
    stages. Portfolio-ready first, public launch when safety features
    (quick exit, verification, rate limits) are complete.
    Why: D3 keeps legal risk low enough that a real launch is realistic,
    and a real product is worth more than a demo, even as a portfolio piece.


D9  [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Links/statuses/notices stored server-side; evidence
    encrypted client-side with a passphrase-derived key.
    Why: the server needs to read links to re-check them, but it never needs
    to read evidence - so evidence stays unreadable to us.

D10 [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Repo layout: Next.js app in /web, extension later in
    /extension.
    Why: room for multiple clients in one repo without restructuring later.

D11 [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Visual design: "Garden" direction (warm oat paper,
    green ink, Vollkorn + Hanken Grotesk, no imagery of people).
    Why: calm and human for people in distress; words, not pictures, carry
    the page. Open to change - the user reviews and asks for changes.

D12 [RETIRED 2026-11-14 by phase 2] Prototype stored cases in browser storage.
    Why: gives a fully clickable prototype today with no backend. To be
    replaced by the server database (D9) in build phase 3.

D13 [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Registered images are fingerprinted ON THE DEVICE.
    The browser computes SHA-256, pHash and the embedding; only fingerprints
    reach the server. D3 still holds: Nivra's server never stores a
    registered original. (Section 13.5-13.9.)
    Why: removes breach and illegal-material risk; same model as StopNCII.

D14 [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Accounts: anonymous by default (Nivra ID +
    passphrase), optional email added later for alerts and recovery. One
    account can hold several cases and protected images.
    Why: keeps D7's low barrier; monitoring alerts need an opt-in contact.

D15 [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Development database: SQLite now, PostgreSQL +
    pgvector in production, same code switched by DATABASE_URL. Docker,
    Redis and a task queue arrive with the discovery phase.
    Why: this machine has no Docker/Postgres; zero-install start.

D16 [DECIDED 2026-11-14] Maritime and Prune are optional adapters, off by
    default, behind an agent-runtime interface and an LLMProvider interface.
    Default: call the model provider directly, run agents in our backend.
    Why: both add third parties with no stated data region or prompt
    retention policy; sensitive case text should not travel further.

12. GLOSSARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

NCII - Non-Consensual Intimate Imagery.
Hash - a short fingerprint computed from a file; the same image gives the same
  hash, but you can't rebuild the image from the hash.
Perceptual hash (pHash, PDQ) - a hash that stays similar even if the image is
  resized, cropped slightly or recompressed, so re-uploads can be matched.
De-indexing - removing a page from search results without removing the page.
Grievance Officer - the person Indian platforms must name to handle complaints.
PWA - Progressive Web App; a website that can be installed like an app.
  Nivra deliberately does NOT do this (see D4).
Responsive - a page layout that adapts to the screen size (phone/laptop).
CDN - Content Delivery Network (e.g. Cloudflare) that sits in front of sites.

13. PLATFORM V2 - FROM PROTOTYPE TO REAL PLATFORM (audit, 2026-11-14)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

13.1 What changed
The team set a bigger vision: users register images they want protected,
Nivra fingerprints them, discovers copies online through authorised sources,
lets the user confirm matches, then drafts, tracks and follows up complaints.
Built with a Python/FastAPI backend and four AI agents (LangGraph).

13.2 Audit - what already works (prototype, frontend only)
Landing, help and 12 platform guides; 6-step case wizard with under-18
redirect; link -> platform recognition; template notices (IT Rules 2021 +
IT Act); anonymous Case ID + passphrase; dashboard with statuses, history
and escalation ladder; quick exit. All verified by clicking through.

13.3 Audit - partial or fake today
Cases live in browser storage only (D12). Platform links, helplines and
legal text are unverified. No server, so no re-checks or reminders. The
"pretend the deadline passed" button is a test tool. No tests exist yet.

13.4 Audit - missing versus the vision
Backend and database; accounts/auth; image registration; SHA-256, pHash
and embedding fingerprints; discovery providers and matching; human review
of matches; evidence store; agents; background jobs; notifications;
data deletion; audit trail; deployment.

13.5 The big conflict: uploading images vs D3
D3 says images never leave the user's device. Registering images for
monitoring seems to need uploads. Storing intimate images on our server is
a breach magnet, and if one shows a minor we'd hold illegal material.
StopNCII solves this by fingerprinting on the device. Decision: see D13.

13.6 Option A - fingerprint on the device (recommended)
The browser computes SHA-256 and pHash locally; only fingerprints are sent.
Embeddings can also run in the browser with a small vision model
(e.g. CLIP via ONNX/transformers.js, a one-off download of ~80-100 MB). The
server never holds a registered original. Matches what the vision asks for:
"fingerprint-only monitoring after registration".

13.7 Option B - transient server processing
The image is uploaded over TLS, fingerprinted in memory and deleted at once;
only fingerprints are kept. Simpler and allows bigger models, but the image
does touch our server, so logs, crashes and memory dumps become risks.

13.8 Option C - encrypted storage of originals
Keep originals encrypted with a retention window so they can be re-processed
when models improve. Most flexible, most dangerous. Not recommended.

13.9 Fingerprints are not harmless either
A perceptual hash or embedding can't rebuild the image, but embeddings can
leak some visual features (inversion attacks exist). They are encrypted at
rest, tied to the account, and deleted when the user revokes registration.

13.10 Discovery reality check
No free, unrestricted reverse-image-search API exists. Google Lens has no
API, Bing's search APIs were retired (2025), TinEye is paid and licensed.
First provider: user-submitted URLs (always allowed). Next: one licensed
provider (e.g. TinEye) once there is a budget and their terms allow it.

13.11 Discovery risk - fetching candidate images
To match a candidate the server must download it. A candidate could be
illegal material (CSAM). Rules: fetch only via allowed providers, respect
robots.txt and rate limits, process in memory, never persist candidate
images, keep only URL + fingerprints + scores. Industry CSAM-hash checks
(e.g. PhotoDNA) need approval and are a later step.

13.12 Match classification (never automatic accusation)
exact (same SHA-256) -> high-confidence (pHash Hamming distance <= ~8 of 64
or embedding cosine >= ~0.92) -> possible (looser thresholds) -> none.
Thresholds are configuration, not code. Every match needs the user's
confirmation before anything is drafted or sent.

13.13 Agents - what they will and won't do
Discovery Coordinator (jobs, providers), Verification Assistant (explains
scores, never decides consent), Complaint Generation (drafts only, user
approves), Follow-up (deadlines, reminders, never assumes success). Agents
see URLs, scores and case text - never image pixels. LLM output is
validated against schemas before use.

13.14 Maritime (maritime.sh) - findings
A hosting platform that runs each agent in its own micro-VM, deployed from
a Dockerfile repo or image; REST API at api.maritime.sh with bearer keys,
Python SDK "maritime", cron and webhook triggers. LangGraph works only as a
custom container. Docs state no data residency, prompt-retention policy or
privacy policy, and logs are kept 12 hours.

13.15 Prune (withprune.com) - findings
A managed LLM gateway: change the base URL, keep your provider keys in its
vault, get spend caps, caching and "Shield" PII guardrails. OpenAI-style
chat completions are documented; native Anthropic Messages support is not
confirmed. No processing region stated; usage metadata kept for billing;
it says it is not SOC 2 certified.

13.16 Recommendation for Maritime and Prune
Both would send sensitive case text to extra third parties with unclear
retention. Plan: build an LLMProvider interface and an agent runtime
interface; default to calling the model provider directly and running
agents inside our own backend; add Prune and Maritime as optional adapters,
switched off by default. Decision: see D16.

13.17 Proposed stack (keep it lean)
FastAPI + SQLAlchemy 2 + Alembic; PostgreSQL with the pgvector extension
instead of a separate Qdrant server (one database, fewer moving parts);
Redis + a task queue only from the discovery phase on; private encrypted
object storage only for evidence the user chooses to keep; LangGraph for
the agents.

13.18 Why pgvector over Qdrant
At our scale (thousands, not billions, of fingerprints) pgvector is fast
enough, keeps fingerprints and permissions in one transactional database,
and removes a service to secure and back up. A vector-store interface lets
us move to Qdrant later if search volume demands it.

13.19 Proposed folder structure
  web/                 existing Next.js app (keeps the Garden design)
  backend/app/         FastAPI app: api/ (routes), models/ (database),
                       schemas/ (validated I/O), services/ (business logic),
                       core/ (config, security, db), fingerprint/, discovery/,
                       agents/, llm/ (provider interface + adapters)
  backend/alembic/     database migrations
  backend/tests/       pytest suite
  extension/           Chrome extension (later)

13.20 Data flow (target)
Browser fingerprints image -> POST fingerprints -> stored per user ->
discovery job (provider gives candidate URLs) -> worker fetches candidate in
memory -> fingerprints compared -> match saved with scores -> user reviews ->
confirmed match becomes an incident -> complaint draft -> user approves and
submits -> follow-up tracks deadline and responses.

13.21 Phase plan (from the vision brief)
P1 audit (this section) - P2 backend foundation: FastAPI, DB, auth, cases API,
frontend wired to it - P3 registration + fingerprints - P4 discovery +
matching + jobs - P5 agents - P6 incidents + evidence + complaints -
P7 monitoring + notifications - P8 production readiness.

14. PHASE 2 - BACKEND FOUNDATION (built and tested 2026-11-14)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

14.1 What was built
A FastAPI backend in backend/ with a real database, anonymous accounts,
login sessions, cases, links, status history, an audit trail and full
account deletion. The website now saves everything through it instead of
browser storage (D12 is retired).

14.2 How the website reaches the backend
The browser only talks to localhost:3000. Next.js forwards /api/* to FastAPI
on port 8000 (a "rewrite" in web/next.config.ts). This keeps the login
cookie first-party and means no cross-site (CORS) setup is needed.

14.3 Backend layout (backend/app)
  main.py          app setup, security headers, safe error messages
  core/config.py   every setting from environment variables (NIVRA_*)
  core/db.py       database engine, UTC-safe timestamp type
  core/security.py passphrase hashing, session tokens, Nivra IDs, encryption
  core/ratelimit.py login/registration rate limiting
  models.py        database tables
  schemas.py       validated request/response shapes
  api/             routes: auth.py, account.py, cases.py, deps.py
  services/        links.py (URL rules, status machine), audit.py

14.4 Tables
users (Nivra ID, Argon2 passphrase hash, optional encrypted email),
sessions (SHA-256 of the token, expiry), cases, case_links (url, host,
status), status_events (timeline), audit_events (who did what, never
content). Image, fingerprint, discovery and complaint tables arrive with
the phases that use them.

14.5 Why Argon2 for passphrases
Argon2id is memory-hard: guessing passphrases from a leaked database needs
lots of memory per guess, which makes GPU cracking far slower than with
plain hashes. Login also spends the same time for unknown IDs, so timing
doesn't reveal which Nivra IDs exist.

14.6 Why sessions are random tokens in httpOnly cookies
A 256-bit random token is set as an httpOnly, SameSite=Lax cookie, so
page scripts can't read it (blocks theft by XSS). The database stores only
its SHA-256, so a database leak doesn't hand out working logins. Logout and
Quick exit delete the session on the server.

14.7 Why the server checks status changes
The buttons only show valid next steps, but the server enforces the rules
too (e.g. a link can't jump from "found" to "removed"). That way a broken or
tampered client can't corrupt a case's history.

14.8 Every query is scoped to the account
Cases and links are always looked up together with the logged-in user's ID,
so guessing another account's case ID returns "not found". Tested
explicitly in tests/test_cases.py.

14.9 Privacy-safe errors and logs
Validation errors name the wrong field but never echo what was typed
(passphrases, links). Unexpected errors log only the error type and route.
The audit trail records actions and IDs, never URLs, emails or notice text.

14.10 User-controlled deletion
"Delete my account and all data" (needs the passphrase again) removes the
account, sessions, cases, links and history. Audit rows stay only as
anonymous counts with no link to the person.

14.11 Optional email, encrypted
PATCH /api/account can store an email for future alerts, encrypted with a
key from NIVRA_DATA_KEY (Fernet). Without that key the feature switches off
instead of storing plaintext. There is no email screen on the website yet.

14.12 The timezone bug we caught
SQLite drops time zones, so timestamps came back without "Z" and browsers
would read them as local time - 5.5 hours off in India. A custom UTCDateTime
column type now always returns UTC. A test caught it before release.

14.13 Settings that must change for production
NIVRA_ENVIRONMENT=production refuses to start unless cookies are Secure,
prototype tools are off and the database is PostgreSQL. Interactive API
docs (/api/docs) exist only in development.

14.14 Tests (backend/tests, 31 passing)
Registration, login, logout, wrong passphrase vs unknown ID, rate limiting,
hashing (nothing stored in plaintext), encrypted email, account deletion,
security headers, case CRUD, cross-account isolation, link validation,
the status state machine, the prototype deadline tool, and a check that
the Alembic migrations build exactly the tables the models describe.

14.15 How to run everything
Terminal 1: cd backend, then .venv/Scripts/python -m uvicorn app.main:app
--reload --port 8000. Terminal 2: cd web, then npm run dev. Open
localhost:3000. Full setup steps are in backend/README.md.

14.16 Still not done after phase 2
No image registration or fingerprints yet (phase 3). No discovery, agents,
notifications or email screen. Rate limiting is per process (moves to Redis
later). Platform data and legal wording still unverified. Not deployed.

15. PHASE 3 - IMAGE REGISTRATION AND FINGERPRINTING (built 2026-11-14)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

15.1 What was built
A "Protect images" page (/protect). The user picks an image on their own
device; the browser computes three fingerprints and sends only those to
Nivra. The image itself is never uploaded (D13). Users can pause, resume or
remove protection, and check a copy they found against their own images.

15.2 The three fingerprints
SHA-256: a fingerprint of the exact file bytes - finds byte-identical
copies only. pHash: a 64-bit "visual" fingerprint that survives resizing,
recompression and small edits. Embedding: 512 numbers from an AI vision
model (CLIP) describing the image - catches crops and heavier edits.

15.3 How pHash works (algorithm "phash-dct-v1")
Shrink the image to 32x32, convert to grey, run a 2-D DCT (the maths behind
JPEG), keep the 64 lowest-frequency values, and set each bit by whether the
value is above their median. Similar-looking images keep most bits, so the
number of differing bits ("Hamming distance") measures similarity.

15.4 Two implementations, one answer
Registered images are hashed in the browser (TypeScript); candidates found
online will be hashed on the server (Python, phase 4). Both must agree.
Shared test vectors (backend/tests/fixtures/phash_vectors.json) are checked
by pytest AND by web/scripts/check-phash.ts.

15.5 The floating-point bug the vectors caught
On symmetric images many DCT values are exactly zero in theory but tiny
noise (1e-13) in practice, and that noise differed between NumPy and
JavaScript, flipping bits. Fix: a value only counts as "above the median"
if it beats it by a tiny relative margin (1e-9). Now all vectors match.

15.6 Real-browser cross-check
The same PNG hashed by the browser and by Python gave identical SHA-256
and identical pHash (distance 0). A 3000x2000 JPEG differed by 2 of 64
bits because browsers and Pillow shrink images differently - well inside
the "high" threshold of 6.

15.7 Why the browser shrinks big images in steps
A canvas that shrinks a huge photo to 32x32 in one jump skips pixels and
gives a noisy result. Halving the size repeatedly first approximates
Pillow's area-averaging resize, keeping browser and server hashes close.

15.8 The AI embedding, on the device
CLIP ViT-B/32 (8-bit quantised) runs in the browser through transformers.js
(@huggingface/transformers 4.3.1, ONNX Runtime Web). It is opt-in: about
90 MB downloads once from Hugging Face and is cached; the image never
leaves the device. On a test machine it took about 19 seconds the first time.

15.9 Swappable model
The server records the model name ("clip-vit-base-patch32-q8") with every
embedding and only compares embeddings from the same model. Changing model
means adding a new name to NIVRA_ALLOWED_EMBEDDING_MODELS - no rewrite.

15.10 Classification rules
exact = same SHA-256. high = pHash distance <= 6, OR pHash <= 12 with
embedding cosine >= 0.95. possible = pHash <= 12, OR embedding cosine
>= 0.92. Otherwise none. Thresholds are settings (NIVRA_PHASH_*,
NIVRA_COSINE_*). Every result is a suggestion for human review.

15.11 What testing taught us about CLIP (important)
A DIFFERENT test image scored 0.95 cosine with the original - CLIP judges
what an image is about, not whether it is the same picture. So an embedding
alone can never make a match "high"; at most "possible". Results: resized
copy = high, cropped copy = possible (pHash alone missed it), different
image = possible (a false alarm that a human dismisses).

15.12 Next step for the embedding (recommended)
Evaluate on a real image-copy-detection benchmark (e.g. DISC21 or
Copydays) and consider a model trained for copy detection, such as SSCD,
instead of CLIP. The swappable design (15.9) exists for exactly this.

15.13 Consent and the under-18 rule
Before choosing an image the user confirms the person was 18+ when it was
taken and that it is them or they are authorised. "No / not sure" routes to
Take It Down, cybercrime.gov.in and 1930 and stops. The server also refuses
registration without both confirmations and records the consent version.

15.14 What the server stores per protected image
A label the user chose (advised not to describe the image), status,
consent version and time, and the fingerprints. Fingerprint values are
never sent back to the browser. Removing protection deletes them; deleting
the account deletes everything.

15.15 Embeddings at rest
Embeddings could leak some visual features (13.9), but app-level encryption
would stop the database searching them. Decision: rely on encrypted storage
at the database/disk level in production, strict per-account access, and
deletion on revoke. Revisit if a vector database is introduced.

15.16 Trauma-informed details
The local preview is blurred by default with a "Show preview" button. The
page states plainly what works today and that automatic web searching
arrives in the next phase - no implied monitoring that doesn't exist yet.

15.17 API added (backend/app/api/images.py)
POST /api/images (register fingerprints), GET /api/images, PATCH
/api/images/{id} (label, pause/resume), PUT /api/images/{id}/embedding (add
the AI fingerprint later), DELETE /api/images/{id} (revoke), POST
/api/images/check (compare a copy with your own images only).

15.18 Tests (57 backend tests passing + TS vector check)
Shared vectors, resize/JPEG/brightness robustness, different images far
apart, transparency handling, classification rules incl. the CLIP cap,
consent required, malformed fingerprints and smuggled image data rejected,
duplicates, pause/resume, revoke, cross-account isolation, account deletion.

15.19 Known limits after phase 3
"Ready for monitoring" means fingerprints are saved; no automatic web
search runs yet (phase 4). CLIP cannot reliably tell a cropped copy from a
similar-looking different photo (15.11). Uvicorn's auto-reload did not
detect file changes in this OneDrive folder - restart the backend manually
after code changes.

16. PHASE 4 - DISCOVERY AND MATCHING (built 2026-11-15)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

16.1 What was built
On /protect, "Check a web page for your images": the user pastes a page or
image link, Nivra visits it in the background, fingerprints every image in
memory, compares them with the user's active protected images, and lists
possible matches for the user to review. Confirmed matches can be added to
a case with one click, which gives them a takedown notice.

16.2 The key consequence of D13 (read this)
Because Nivra never holds the original image, it can't send it to a
reverse-image-search service (TinEye, Google Lens etc. all need the actual
picture). So discovery works the other way round: Nivra visits pages and
compares what it finds against fingerprints. Decision on external search:
D18 (open).

16.3 Provider interface (backend/app/discovery/providers.py)
Every source of candidate images implements DiscoveryProvider.discover().
Today there is one: UserSubmittedUrlProvider ("user_url"). Future sources
(scheduled re-checks, a platform API, a licensed search service) are new
classes registered in PROVIDERS; the job runner does not change. Providers
that would need the original image are flagged and not allowed.

16.4 The safe fetcher (backend/app/discovery/fetch.py)
The only code that touches the web. Letting a server fetch user-supplied
links is a classic attack route ("SSRF"): someone could point it at
internal services or cloud-metadata addresses (169.254.169.254) to steal
secrets. The fetcher is built to make that impossible.

16.5 How SSRF is blocked
The hostname is resolved first; if ANY address is private, loopback,
link-local or reserved, the fetch is refused before anything is sent. The
request then goes to that exact IP (with TLS still verifying the real
hostname), so DNS can't be switched mid-request ("DNS rebinding").
Redirects are followed by hand, max 3, re-checking every hop.

16.6 Other fetch limits
Only http/https on ports 80/443. 10-second timeouts. Pages max 2 MB,
images max 15 MB, enforced while streaming. Images over 40 megapixels are
refused ("decompression bombs"). Only JPEG/PNG/WebP/GIF/BMP are decoded;
SVG is skipped (it can contain scripts).

16.7 Being a good web citizen
Our bot identifies itself ("NivraBot/0.1"), honours robots.txt (missing =
allowed, server error = don't crawl), pauses 0.5 s between requests,
stops on 401/403 (login walls) and 429 (slow down) and never tries to get
around them - no logins, no CAPTCHA solving. Each account can run at most
20 checks per hour.

16.8 What happens to found images
Candidate bytes exist only in memory while being fingerprinted, then are
dropped. Only URLs, which protected image matched, the classification and
the scores are saved - and only for exact/high/possible results. A
different image leaves no trace. Tests check that no bytes are stored.

16.9 Matching rules
Same rules as section 15.10, against the user's ACTIVE images only - so
"Pause monitoring" really pauses matching. A page checked again updates its
existing matches instead of duplicating them, and keeps the user's review.

16.10 Why the server has no AI embedding for candidates yet
To compare embeddings, the server would need the exact same CLIP model as
the browser (ONNX, same preprocessing). It is deferred: CLIP alone can only
ever produce a "possible" (15.11), and a copy-detection model may replace
it (15.12). Server matching uses SHA-256 and pHash today.

16.11 Background jobs without Redis [DECIDED - D17]
Jobs live in the discovery_jobs table and a worker claims the oldest
queued one with a conditional UPDATE (so two workers can't take the same
job). In development the worker runs as a thread inside the API; in
production run "python -m app.discovery.worker". Stuck jobs are retried up
to 3 times. Celery + Redis can replace this later behind the same API.

16.12 Human in the loop
Every match starts "pending". The user chooses "Yes, this is my image" or
"No, not mine" (dismissed matches can be restored). Only confirmed matches
can be added to a case, and adding one puts the PAGE URL (what platforms
need) into the case as a link.

16.13 Why found images are never shown
Nivra doesn't keep them, and loading them straight from the site would
reveal the user's visit (possibly to the person who posted them). The UI
offers "Open the page in a new tab" with that warning, and suggests a
trusted friend can check instead.

16.14 Local testing override
NIVRA_DISCOVERY_ALLOW_PRIVATE_HOSTS=true lets the server fetch localhost /
private addresses so you can test with a page on your own machine (use
127.0.0.1 links). The server refuses to start in production with it on.

16.15 Tested in the real browser
A local test page with 4 images: the exact copy and the resized copy were
flagged (exact, very likely), the different image left no record, and the
missing image was counted as skipped. Confirming one and adding it to a new
case put the page link into that case. In normal mode the same local page
and the cloud-metadata address were both refused.

16.16 Bugs testing caught
An ftp:// link got a misleading "robots.txt" message because robots was
checked before the scheme; and the metadata address was refused by the
robots step instead of the "private network" rule. Both fixed: address and
scheme checks now run first, before even robots.txt is requested.

16.17 API added (backend/app/api/discovery.py)
POST /api/discovery/jobs, GET /api/discovery/jobs, GET
/api/discovery/jobs/{id}, GET /api/matches (?status_filter=), POST
/api/matches/{id}/review, POST /api/matches/{id}/add-to-case.

16.18 Tests (90 backend tests passing)
Public/private IP rules incl. IPv6 and mapped addresses, SSRF refusals
with nothing sent, pinning to the resolved IP, redirect re-checks, size
caps, login walls, robots.txt, HTML extraction, decompression bombs, full
jobs with a fake website, re-runs, paused images, failures, rate limits,
review and add-to-case, cross-account isolation, deletion, the worker.

16.19 Still not done after phase 4
No automatic searching beyond pages the user gives (see D18). No scheduled
re-checks (phase 7). No server-side embedding. The rate limiter and the
inline worker are single-process (fine for development, not for scale).

D17 [DECIDED 2026-11-15] Background jobs use a database-backed queue and a
    simple worker, not Redis + Celery (yet).
    Why: no Docker on the dev machine, low job volume, one less service to
    secure. The worker module can be swapped for Celery later.

D18 [OPEN 2026-11-15] How (or whether) to search the wider web.
    Options: (a) stay fingerprint-only - user-submitted pages plus later
    scheduled re-checks of known pages; (b) let a user explicitly send ONE
    image to a licensed reverse-search API (e.g. TinEye, paid) from their
    browser, an informed exception to D13; (c) apply to industry hash-
    sharing programmes (StopNCII-style) as a partner. Team to decide.
