================================================================================
                        NIVRA - THE MASTER GUIDE
================================================================================

What this file is: the single place to understand everything about Nivra -
what it is, why it exists, every architectural choice, why it was made, what
alternatives were rejected, and what each choice led to. It is a living
document: every decision we make gets added here.

How to read it: each idea is a short paragraph of 3-4 lines. Sections go from
"why" (problem, landscape) to "what" (product) to "how" (architecture), and end
with a Decision Log that records every choice in order with its status.

Status tags used throughout:
  [DECIDED]   - agreed by the team, build on it
  [PROPOSED]  - recommended, waiting for team sign-off
  [OPEN]      - still a question, no recommendation yet
  [REJECTED]  - considered and turned down (kept so we remember why)

Last updated: 2026-11-14 (project kickoff)


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1.1 What happens to victims
Someone's photo - often intimate, sometimes ordinary - gets uploaded online
without their consent. It may be on a social network, a porn site, a Telegram
channel, or a forum. The person usually finds out late, feels panic and shame,
and has no idea who to contact or what their rights are.

1.2 Why removal is hard today
Every platform has a different reporting form, different rules, and different
response times. Laws differ by country. Offshore sites ignore requests. Victims
must repeatedly look at the content to report it, which re-traumatises them.
Even after removal, the same image gets re-uploaded elsewhere.

1.3 The core idea of Nivra
Nivra is a guided helper that takes a victim from "I just found my photo
online" to "it's been taken down and I'm watching for re-uploads". It finds
the right reporting channel, writes the legal notice for them, tracks every
link, and escalates when platforms don't respond.

1.4 Kinds of non-consensual images in scope [OPEN]
(a) Intimate images shared without consent (NCII, "revenge porn").
(b) Morphed / AI-generated deepfakes using someone's face.
(c) Ordinary photos misused - fake profiles, catfishing, doxxing, photos of
children posted by others. Scope decision is pending - see Decision Log D1.

2. THE EXISTING LANDSCAPE (what already exists and how Nivra differs)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.1 StopNCII.org
Run by SWGfL with Meta's tech. The user's device computes a hash (digital
fingerprint) of the image; only the hash is shared with partner platforms
(Meta, TikTok, Reddit, Bing and others), which block matching uploads. Great
for prevention on big platforms, useless for small/offshore sites.

2.2 Take It Down (NCMEC)
Same hash idea, but for images of people taken when they were under 18. Nivra
must route minors here instead of handling their images itself - possessing
such images is a serious crime even for a helper service.

2.3 Platform and search-engine forms
Google, Bing, Meta, X, Reddit etc. each have NCII/privacy removal forms.
Google's de-indexing form is especially powerful: even if a site refuses to
remove the image, it can disappear from search results, which is where most
people would find it.

2.4 Where the gap is (Nivra's reason to exist)
Existing tools are scattered, each covers one platform or one type of victim,
and none tracks a case end-to-end. Nivra's value is orchestration: one place
that knows every channel, writes every notice, follows up, escalates, and
watches for re-uploads.

3. THE LEGAL GROUND WE STAND ON
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.1 India - IT Rules 2021
Rule 3(2)(b) requires intermediaries to remove content showing a person's
private area, nudity, sexual acts or impersonation (incl. morphed images)
within 24 hours of a complaint by that person or someone on their behalf.
Every significant platform must publish a Grievance Officer contact.

3.2 India - escalation routes
If a platform ignores the grievance, users can appeal to the Grievance
Appellate Committee (GAC). Criminal complaints go to cybercrime.gov.in or the
1930 helpline. IT Act Sections 66E and 67/67A cover these offences.

3.3 USA - TAKE IT DOWN Act (2025)
Makes publishing NCII (including AI deepfakes) a federal crime and requires
covered platforms to remove reported NCII within 48 hours of a valid request.
DMCA copyright notices also work when the victim took the photo themselves
(a selfie's copyright belongs to the person who took it).

3.4 EU / UK
GDPR's right to erasure and the EU Digital Services Act's notice-and-action
system apply to EU users. The UK Online Safety Act makes sharing intimate
images an offence and places duties on platforms.

3.5 Our legal limits
Nivra is not a law firm. We generate templates and explain options; we do not
give legal advice. Notices are sent in the user's name with their explicit
consent. False takedown notices can carry penalties, so verification matters.

4. KEY CHALLENGES (and how we plan to handle each)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

4.1 Finding where the image is
There is no free, legal, public API for reverse image search at scale. Google
Lens has no API, Bing's search APIs were retired in 2025, TinEye's API is paid,
and crawling adult sites ourselves is expensive and legally grey. Plan: start
with user-supplied links plus assisted reverse search (see 5.1).

4.2 Holding sensitive images is a liability
If victims upload intimate images to our server, a breach would be a disaster,
and if any image shows a minor we'd be holding illegal material. Plan: images
never leave the user's device. We compute hashes and do any processing in the
browser. The server only stores links, statuses and hashes.

4.3 Misuse of the tool itself
A stalker could use a "find my photos" feature to find someone else's photos,
or a bad actor could file fake takedowns against legitimate content. Plan:
no open face-search, notices sent only for content the user attests is
theirs, rate limits, and (later) identity checks for automated features.

4.4 Every platform is different
Reporting channels are web forms, emails, in-app buttons - almost never APIs.
Auto-filling forms breaks platform ToS and hits CAPTCHAs. Plan: a curated
Platform Directory + pre-filled notices the user submits in one click, and
direct emails to grievance/abuse addresses where they exist.

4.5 Non-cooperative / offshore sites
Some sites ignore every request. Plan: an escalation ladder - site, then the
hosting provider, the CDN (e.g. Cloudflare), the domain registrar, search
engine de-indexing, and finally law enforcement. Each step is automatable.

4.6 Re-uploads
Removed images reappear. Plan: periodic re-checking of known links, plus
hash registration with StopNCII for participating platforms, plus (later)
monitoring for new copies.

4.7 Evidence vs. removal conflict
Police and lawyers need evidence, but the goal is removal. If we remove first,
evidence is lost. Plan: an Evidence Locker that captures URL, timestamp,
screenshot and hash before takedown, encrypted on the user's side.

4.8 Trauma and emotional safety
Users may be in crisis. Plan: trauma-informed design - calm language, blurred
previews by default, never forcing the user to view the content, a quick-exit
button, helpline links on every screen, and an option to let a trusted friend
handle the case.

4.9 Deepfakes and morphed images
A morphed image isn't a copy of an original photo, so hash matching fails.
Detection needs face-based matching, which is biometric data and sensitive
under India's DPDP Act and laws like Illinois' BIPA. Plan: handle deepfakes
through the same takedown flow first; face matching only later, opt-in.

4.10 Cost and scale
We are a small team. Paid search APIs, crawling and ML inference cost money.
Plan: build an MVP that is almost free to run (user links + notice generation
+ tracking) and add expensive discovery features only once the core works.

5. APPROACHES CONSIDERED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

5.1 Discovery (finding the images)
  A. User-provided links - user pastes URLs they found. Zero legal risk, cheap,
     works today. Limitation: only finds what the user already knows.
  B. Assisted reverse search - the user picks an image on their own device and
     we open Google Lens / Yandex / TinEye with it. They search, we organise
     the results. Note: this does send the image to that search engine, and
     the user must be told so.
  C. Hash registration - integrate with / redirect to StopNCII rather than
     build our own hash network, since platforms already trust it.
  D. Own crawler + perceptual hash matching - the "dream" feature, but costly
     and legally risky. Later phase, if ever.
  E. Face search (PimEyes-style) - highest misuse risk. Only ever as a
     verified self-search, and probably never in MVP.
  Recommendation [PROPOSED]: A + B + C for MVP. D/E deferred.

5.2 Takedown (getting it removed)
  A. Notice generator - picks the strongest legal basis (IT Rules, DMCA,
     TAKE IT DOWN, GDPR, platform policy) and fills a template.
  B. Platform Directory - for each platform/host: reporting URL, email,
     grievance officer, legal basis, expected response time.
  C. Escalation ladder - automatic suggestions when deadlines pass.
  D. Case tracker - each link has a status; we re-check if it's still live.
  E. Sending on the user's behalf - emails from Nivra with consent. Needs
     good email deliverability and clear authorisation wording.
  Recommendation [PROPOSED]: A + B + C + D for MVP, E soon after.

6. PRODUCT FORM FACTOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

6.1 Web app / PWA - core product [PROPOSED]
Works on any phone without installing anything, which matters because most
victims (especially in India) will find Nivra on mobile, often in a hurry.
A Progressive Web App can later be "installed" to the home screen and look
like a native app.

6.2 Chrome extension - companion [PROPOSED, phase 2]
Right-click any image on any page -> "Report with Nivra". It captures the
URL, takes an evidence screenshot, hashes the image locally, and adds it to
the user's case. Excellent for collecting links, but desktop-only, so it
cannot be the main product.

6.3 Agent skill - not the product [REJECTED as main form]
An AI-assistant skill alone would feel low effort and puts a sensitive flow
inside someone else's product. However, an AI agent INSIDE Nivra's backend
(writing notices, finding host contacts, reading platform replies) is
valuable and is planned.

6.4 Messaging bot (Telegram / WhatsApp) [OPEN, later]
A lot of leaked content in India spreads on Telegram. A bot that accepts a
forwarded link and opens a case meets users where the harm happens. Worth
considering after the web app is stable.

6.5 Native mobile app [REJECTED for now]
App-store review, two codebases, and installation friction. The PWA covers
the same need until we have real users asking for native features.

7. FEATURE IDEAS (beyond the obvious)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

7.1 Quick exit button - one tap jumps to a harmless page (e.g. weather) and
clears the tab, in case someone walks in on the user.

7.2 Anonymous mode - open a case with no email/phone, just a passphrase.
Lowers the barrier for people afraid of being identified.

7.3 Trusted helper mode - the victim can hand the case to a friend, family
member or NGO worker who does the work without the victim seeing the content.

7.4 Evidence Locker - encrypted on the user's device (we can't read it),
exportable as a PDF evidence pack with timestamps and SHA-256 hashes for
police or lawyers.

7.5 Re-upload watch - Nivra periodically re-checks removed links and alerts
the user if content comes back.

7.6 Platform response scoreboard - aggregated, anonymous stats of which
platforms remove content fastest. Useful for users and for advocacy.

7.7 Multilingual - Hindi and other Indian languages, both for the UI and for
notices where local language helps.

7.8 NGO / legal-aid partners - referral links to support organisations,
counselling, and pro-bono lawyers built into the flow.

7.9 Prevention tools [later] - helping people protect photos before posting
(e.g. perturbation techniques that make AI morphing harder).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. PROPOSED ARCHITECTURE (MVP) [PROPOSED - not final]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

8.1 Guiding principle: data minimisation
The server should know as little as possible. No images, only links, hashes,
statuses and the minimum contact info needed. If we're ever breached, the
leaked data should be close to useless to an attacker.

8.2 Frontend
Next.js (React) + TypeScript + Tailwind CSS, delivered as a PWA. Next.js
gives us routing, server rendering for fast first load on slow phones, and
API routes in the same project. TypeScript catches bugs early.

8.3 Client-side image processing
Perceptual hashing (pHash / PDQ) and encryption run in the browser using
WebAssembly and the Web Crypto API. This is what lets us promise "your images
never leave your device".

8.4 Backend
Start inside Next.js API routes to keep one codebase. If we add heavy image or
ML work later, a separate Python (FastAPI) service can be added for that part
only. Background jobs handle link re-checks and reminders.

8.5 Database
PostgreSQL (e.g. via Supabase) for cases, links, platform directory and
statuses. Relational data fits well: a case has many links, each link belongs
to a platform, each platform has many reporting channels.

8.6 AI layer
An LLM (Claude) drafts notices tailored to platform + law, classifies a URL
to its platform/host, and later reads platform replies to update status.
Images are never sent to the LLM - only text and URLs.

8.7 Email
A transactional email service (e.g. Resend / Postmark) for sending notices
on the user's behalf and for status updates, with proper SPF/DKIM so mails
don't land in spam.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. FRONTEND DESIGN DIRECTION (draft, to be refined with the design prompt)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

9.1 Tone - calm, private, in control. Soft neutral colours, no stock images
of people, no alarming red. The user should feel safe the moment the page
loads.

9.2 Main screens
Landing -> "Start a case" wizard (what happened -> where is it -> evidence ->
notices -> submit) -> Case dashboard (each link with status) -> Platform
guides -> Help & resources.

9.3 Safety UX - quick exit always visible, blurred thumbnails by default,
plain-language explanations, never more than one decision per screen.

11. GLOSSARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

NCII - Non-Consensual Intimate Imagery.
Hash - a short fingerprint computed from a file; the same image gives the same
  hash, but you can't rebuild the image from the hash.
Perceptual hash (pHash, PDQ) - a hash that stays similar even if the image is
  resized, cropped slightly or recompressed, so re-uploads can be matched.
De-indexing - removing a page from search results without removing the page.
Grievance Officer - the person Indian platforms must name to handle complaints.
PWA - Progressive Web App; a website that can be installed like an app.
CDN - Content Delivery Network (e.g. Cloudflare) that sits in front of sites.
